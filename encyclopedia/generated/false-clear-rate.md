A d by d matrix A sends x to Ax. The matrices in this book are almost all symmetric, meaning the entry in row i and column j equals the entry in row j and column i.  A projection onto a direction u of length one sends x to the multiple of u that is closest to x. That multiple is the dot product of x with u, times u. The number itself, the dot product with u, is the coordinate of x along u.  $$\operatorname{proj}_u(x)=(u\cdot x)\,u,\qquad \|u\|=1. \tag{0.2}$$  A subspace is a set of directions closed under addition and scaling. The set of all vectors along u is a one-dimensional subspace. The set of all vectors in a plane through the origin is a two-dimensional subspace. The rank of a matrix is the dimension of the subspace its outputs fill. A rank-one matrix sends every input to a multiple of one fixed direction.  *Example.* Let u = (0.6, 0.8), which has length one. Projecting x = (3, 4) onto u gives coordinate 1.8 + 3.2 = 5, so the projection is 5u = (3, 4). Projecting x = (4, 3) onto u gives 2.4 + 2.4 = 4.8, so the projection is (2.88, 3.84).  *Used in.* Section 0.5, where a read operator is a weighted sum of projections. Chapters 3, 4, and 11.  ## 0.3 Variance in many dimensions  For one column, the variance is the average squared distance from the mean. For d columns, the covariance matrix is the d by d table whose diagonal entries are each column's variance and whose off-diagonal entries measure how two columns move together. It is symmetric. Write it as $\Sigma$.  The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. The trace of a covariance matrix is the total variance across all columns. Two datasets can have the same total variance and distribute it across columns completely differently.  $$\Sigma_{ij}=\mathbb E\big[(x_i-\mu_i)(x_j-\mu_j)\big],\qquad \operatorname{tr}\Sigma=\sum_{i}\Sigma_{ii}. \tag{0.3}$$  *Example.* A dataset with variance 0.3 in the first coordinate and 1.7 in the second, and no covariance between them, has covariance matrix with diagonal (0.3, 1.7) and trace 2.0. A dataset with the diagonal reversed, (1.7, 0.3), also has trace 2.0. Chapter 3 shows a reader that ranks these two 4.1 to one.  The variance along a direction $u$ of length one is $u^{\top}\Sigma u$, the dot product of $u$ with $\Sigma u$. It is the variance of the coordinates along u. For the first dataset and u = (1, 0) it is 0.3. For u at 15 degrees from the first axis, u = (0.966, 0.259), it is 0.966 squared times 0.3 plus 0.259 squared times 1.7, which is 0.280 + 0.114 = 0.394.  $$\operatorname{Var}(u\cdot x)=u^{\top}\Sigma\,u. \tag{0.4}$$  ![Figure 0.1](../figures/ch00_fig1.svg)  *Figure 0.1. Two covariances with the same trace, 2.0, and a reader at 15 degrees. The variance the reader sees is 0.394 along the first and 1.606 along the second.*  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.2 and chapter 4 throughout. Whenever the book writes the trace of one matrix times another, it means a weighted total variance, with the weights given by the first matrix.  ## 0.4 Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and the spectrum  A symmetric d by d matrix has d special directions, its eigenvectors, along which it acts as pure scaling. Along eigenvector v with eigenvalue lambda, the matrix sends v to lambda v. The eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix can be chosen orthogonal to each other, and the eigenvalues of a covariance matrix are never negative. The list of eigenvalues is called the spectrum.  $$\Sigma\,v_i=\lambda_i v_i,\qquad \Sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i\,v_i v_i^{\top},\qquad v_i\cdot v_j=0\ (i\ne j). \tag{0.5}$$  For a covariance matrix, the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the direction of largest variance, and the eigenvalue is that variance. That is principal component analysis. TSK Appendix B derives it [@tan2019]. The book uses the vocabulary as follows.  **Isotropic** means every direction has the same variance. All eigenvalues are equal. **Anisotropic** means they differ. A spectrum is **concentrated** when a few eigenvalues carry most of the total.  **Whitening** rescales the data so that its covariance becomes the identity matrix. It divides each eigenvector direction by the square root of its eigenvalue. After whitening every direction has variance one, and distances no longer favour the high-variance directions.  $$x_{\mathrm w}=\Sigma^{-1/2}(x-\mu),\qquad \Sigma^{-1/2}=\sum_i\lambda_i^{-1/2}\,v_i v_i^{\top}. \tag{0.6}$$  **Effective rank** answers the question of how many directions a matrix really uses, without choosing a cutoff. It is the square of the sum of the eigenvalues divided by the sum of the squared eigenvalues. It equals one when a single eigenvalue carries everything and equals d when all eigenvalues are equal.  $$r_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\big(\sum_i\lambda_i\big)^{2}}{\sum_i\lambda_i^{2}}. \tag{0.7}$$  *Example.* Eigenvalues (4, 1, 0, 0). Sum is 5, sum of squares is 17, effective rank is 25 over 17, or 1.47. The matrix mostly uses one direction and partly a second. Eigenvalues (1, 1, 1, 1) give effective rank 16 over 4, or 4.  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.3, where the eigenvectors of a graph's Laplacian give the spectral embedding. Chapter 4 sections 4.2 and 4.5. Chapter 11 section 11.7.  ## 0.5 Reading a vector, and the read operator  A consumer is any computation that takes a vector in and gives a number out. A classifier's score for one class. A ranking function's score for one candidate. A regression's prediction. Call the number C(x).  The **sensitivity** of the consumer at x is the vector of partial derivatives of C with respect to each coordinate. It is also called the gradient and written g. It points in the direction in which a small change to x moves the output most. A coordinate the consumer never uses has a zero entry in g.  Sensitivity can be measured without knowing the formula for C. Change coordinate i of x by a small amount h, evaluate C again, and divide the change in output by h. That is a **finite difference**, and doing it once per coordinate costs d evaluations of C. Doing it in both directions, plus and minus h, costs 2d and is more accurate. Chapter 11 section 11.7 says why 2d is the price at one operating point for a probe confined to the directions it chooses, and that whether many cheaper points can average their way back to the population operator was measured within a budget range, not proved.  $$g_j\;\approx\;\frac{C(x+h\,e_j)-C(x-h\,e_j)}{2h},\qquad j=1,\dots,d. \tag{0.8}$$  The **read operator** of a consumer summarizes its sensitivity over a whole dataset. For each row, form the $d$ by $d$ matrix whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $g_i g_j$. This is the outer product of $g$ with itself. Average those matrices over the rows. The result, written $P_C$, is symmetric, its eigenvectors are the directions the consumer is most sensitive to on average, and its eigenvalues say how sensitive. Directions the consumer never reads have eigenvalue zero. The eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalue span the **read subspace**. $P_C$ is an average of local, linearized sensitivities. Each local operator is positive semidefinite, so squared sensitivities cannot cancel in the average, and the kernel of the average is the intersection of the local kernels over the rows, up to sets of rows of measure zero. A direction with eigenvalue zero in $\bar P_{C,\mu}$ is therefore locally unread at almost every row of the workload, for a nonlinear consumer as much as for a linear one. What the average does not give is a global statement about pairs of inputs, and section 0.6 says what that costs. Chapter 2 section 2.2 keeps the pointwise operator, equation 0.11 evaluated at one row, apart from this average, and the book says which it means wherever the difference matters. The average is also taken over a particular set of rows, a dataset or a workload, and depends on which. Where that matters the book writes $\bar P_{C,\mu}$ with $\mu$ the distribution the rows were drawn from, and a read operator measured on one footprint is a claim about that footprint.  $$P_C=\mathbb E\!\left[g\,g^{\top}\right],\qquad g=\nabla C(x). \tag{0.9}$$  The book's central quantity is the trace of $P_C$ times $M_\delta$, where $M_\delta$ is the second-moment matrix of an error, the average over rows of the error's outer product with itself. The identity below is exact, with no centering, for a fixed read operator, which is the linear case. For a nonlinear consumer whose error depends on the row, the first-order distortion is the average over rows of $\delta(x)^{\top}P_C(x)\delta(x)$, and the trace of the averaged operator times the averaged second moment is a factorized surrogate of it, exact when the operator and the error's outer product are uncorrelated across rows and otherwise an approximation, which the book uses because it is what the instruments of chapter 11 can measure. When the error has mean zero, $M_\delta$ is also the error's covariance, written $\Sigma_\delta$, and the book writes $\Sigma_\delta$ only where an error is centered by construction. A reconstruction error is not centered merely to remove its bias, since the consumer feels the bias too, and a reader who wants the covariance form must add the mean's own term, $\mu_\delta^{\top}P_C\mu_\delta$. The trace of $M_\delta$ is the mean squared vector norm of the error, which is $d$ times the per-coordinate mean squared error a report usually quotes. By section 0.3, that is the total variance of the error weighted by how much the consumer reads each direction. It is the error as the consumer experiences it, to first order and in the factorized form the next paragraph qualifies. Setting P_C to the identity matrix weights every direction equally and recovers plain total squared error. The book calls that the **identity reader**.  $$d_O=\operatorname{tr}(P_C\,M_\delta)=\mathbb E\!\left[\delta^{\top}P_C\,\delta\right],\qquad M_\delta=\mathbb E\!\left[\delta\delta^{\top}\right],\qquad P_C=I\ \Rightarrow\ d_O=\operatorname{tr}M_\delta. \tag{0.10}$$  The identity in equation 0.10, and its identity-reader case, are machine-checked in Lean in appendix C.  *Example.* A consumer that reads only the first coordinate has $g=(1,0)$ everywhere, $P_C$ is the matrix with a single one in the top-left corner, and $\operatorname{tr}(P_C\Sigma)$ is the variance of the first coordinate alone. A consumer that reads the 15-degree direction of section 0.3 has $P_C$ equal to the outer product of $(0.966, 0.259)$ with itself, and $\operatorname{tr}(P_C\Sigma)$ is the 0.394 computed there.  When the consumer outputs a vector rather than a number, the gradient becomes a matrix of partial derivatives called the Jacobian, and the read operator is the average of the Jacobian transposed times the Jacobian. The book mentions the Jacobian by name only in chapter 2. Everything else uses the scalar case. The $G$ in equation 0.11 is the local metric on the consumer's output, a positive semidefinite matrix that says how a small change in the output is scored, and it exists only when the observer's output metric has a local quadratic representation at that output. A dataset-level loss, accuracy, F1, precision, a rank correlation, or dollars lost, is not such a matrix and cannot be inserted into the formula. For an observer scored by such a loss the read operator is computed on the score the loss is applied to, with $G$ the identity, which is the scalar case of equation 0.9, and the loss enters through the decision the score feeds.  $$P_C=J^{\top}G\,J,\qquad J=\frac{\partial C}{\partial x}. \tag{0.11}$$  *Used in.* Everywhere. The definition is repeated in chapter 2 with more care.  ## 0.6 Quotients, or deciding what not to see  Take a set of objects and a rule for when two of them count as the same. The rule groups the set into classes. The **quotient** is the set of classes. A quotient throws away exactly the differences the rule declares irrelevant.  $$x\sim_C x'\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad x-x'\in\ker P_C. \tag{0.12}$$  *Example.* Take all nonzero vectors and declare two the same when one is a positive multiple of the other. Each class is a direction, and the quotient is the set of directions, which is the unit sphere. The angle between two directions is a distance on this quotient, and cosine similarity is the similarity score that reports it, since the angle is the arccosine of the cosine. It cannot see length because length is what the rule discarded.  Every similarity measure in chapter 3 induces a distance on some quotient, and the book keeps the two words apart. A similarity is a score that grows with nearness, and its distance is the one it induces, the angle for cosine, one minus the correlation for correlation, one minus the Jaccard coefficient for Jaccard, each on the quotient that discards what the measure ignores. Correlation distance lives on the quotient that discards shift and scale, and Jaccard distance on the quotient that discards joint absence. When the book says a consumer induces a quotient, it means the consumer's read operator declares two inputs the same when they differ only in directions of eigenvalue zero. That equivalence is exact for an affine consumer with a fixed output geometry, and it names one of three things the book keeps apart. Exact equivalence is that the consumer's outputs at $x$ and $x'$ are the same under its output metric. A local null direction is one in the kernel of $P_C(x)$ at a row, unread there. A workload-common null direction is one in the kernel of $\bar P_{C,\mu}$, unread at almost every row of the workload, since the kernel of the average is the intersection of the local kernels. For a nonlinear consumer the third does not deliver the first. Cosine similarity is the case to hold in mind. At every row it ignores the radial direction, so each local operator has a kernel, but the radial direction turns with the row, the average of those operators can have no kernel at all, and cosine still identifies every ray. The quotient the instruments of chapter 11 measure is the workload-common one.  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.1, chapter 11 section 11.1.  ## 0.7 Bits, budgets, and where to spend them  Storing a number to more precision costs more bits. For a uniform quantizer at high rate, where the rounding error is spread evenly across a step, each extra bit halves the rounding error, so the squared error falls by a factor of four per bit. Coarse quantizers, codebooks with unequal steps, and errors that are not spread evenly depart from that rule, and chapter 4 reports where a measured allocation departed from the formula that rests on it. A compressor with a budget of B bits per row must decide how many bits each of d directions gets.  Take the directions to be the eigenvectors of the data's covariance, with variances $v_i$, and let $s_i=u_i^{\top}P_C u_i$ be the consumer's sensitivity along the $i$th of them, so that both numbers are read in one basis. The consumer's error from rounding direction $i$ with $b_i$ bits is about $s_i v_i$ times four to the power of $-b_i$. The total error is the sum over directions, because the directions are uncorrelated and their errors add. Minimizing the total subject to the bits summing to $B$ has a closed form. Direction $i$ gets half the base-two logarithm of $s_i v_i$ divided by a constant, or zero bits if that would be negative. The constant is set so the bits add up to $B$.  $$D(b)=\sum_i s_i v_i\,4^{-b_i},\qquad b_i=\max\!\Big(0,\ \tfrac12\log_2\frac{s_i v_i}{\theta}\Big),\qquad \sum_i b_i=B. \tag{0.13}$$  ![Figure 0.2](../figures/ch00_fig2.svg)  *Figure 0.2. Water-filling three directions with sensitivity-weighted variances 8, 2, and 0.1 at a budget of 3 bits. The water level is 0.5. The third direction sits under the water and receives no bits.*  This is called **water-filling**, because if you draw each direction's $s_i v_i$ as a column and pour a fixed amount of water over the diagram, the water level is the constant, and the columns below the surface get no bits. The name comes from the same calculation for radio channels, where the columns are signal power. Here the columns are sensitivity times variance, and that is the whole difference between compressing for reconstruction and compressing for a consumer.  *Example.* Three directions with $s_i v_i$ equal to 8, 2, and 0.1 and a budget of 3 bits. Water-filling gives about 2 bits to the first, 1 to the second, and 0 to the third, which sits under the water. Uniform allocation would give 1 each and waste a bit on the direction the consumer barely reads.  *Used in.* Chapter 4 section 4.2, chapter 11 section 11.5.  ## 0.8 Comparing rankings, and scoring a classifier  Many consumers produce rankings rather than numbers. Two rankings of the same items can be compared without caring about the scores that produced them.  **Spearman correlation** replaces each item's score with its rank, then computes the ordinary correlation between the two rank lists. It is one when the rankings agree exactly and negative one when they are reversed.  $$\rho_S=1-\frac{6\sum_i d_i^{2}}{n(n^{2}-1)},\qquad d_i=\text{difference of the two ranks of item } i. \tag{0.14}$$  **Kendall correlation** looks at every pair of items and asks whether the two rankings order the pair the same way. It is the fraction of agreeing pairs minus the fraction of disagreeing pairs. It is also one for perfect agreement and negative one for reversal, and it is easier to bound from below, which is why chapter 11's certificate uses it.  $$\tau=\frac{\#\{\text{concordant pairs}\}-\#\{\text{discordant pairs}\}}{n(n-1)/2}. \tag{0.15}$$  *Example.* Five items ranked (1, 2, 3, 4, 5) by one method and (1, 3, 2, 4, 5) by another. Of the ten pairs, one pair, items 2 and 3, is ordered differently. Kendall is nine tenths minus one tenth, or 0.8. Spearman is 0.9.  **AUROC**, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve, scores a classifier that outputs a number per item when the truth is one of two classes. It equals the probability that a randomly chosen positive item scores higher than a randomly chosen negative one. A random scorer gets 0.5. A perfect one gets 1.0. It does not depend on where a threshold is placed.  $$\mathrm{AUROC}=\Pr\big[s^{+}>s^{-}\big]\ +\ \tfrac12\Pr\big[s^{+}=s^{-}\big]. \tag{0.16}$$  *Example.* Positives score (0.9, 0.7, 0.4). Negatives score (0.6, 0.3). Of the six positive-negative pairs, the positive scores higher in five. AUROC is five sixths, or 0.83.  Every rank statistic has a **chance level**, the value a random ranking would achieve on average. For correlations it is zero. For AUROC it is 0.5. For the overlap between a recovered subspace and a true one it is the ratio of the subspace dimension to the ambient dimension, and chapter 11 prints that ratio beside every reading.  *Used in.* Chapter 8 sections 8.5 and 8.6, chapter 11 sections 11.3 and 11.8, chapter 3 section 3.3.  ## 0.9 Uncertainty, nulls, and multiple comparisons  A number computed from a sample would come out differently on a different sample. The book reports that variation in four ways, and the reader should recognize each.  A **confidence interval** is a range that would contain the true value in a stated fraction of repeated samples, usually 95 percent. When the book writes a number followed by an interval, the interval is the range and the number is the sample estimate.  A **bootstrap** estimates that interval by resampling the data with replacement many times and recomputing the number each time. A **paired bootstrap** resamples the same rows for two methods at once, so that the interval is on their difference and the sampling variation they share cancels out.  A **standard error** is the spread of the estimate across repeated samples. A difference of z standard errors is reported as z sigma. A **p-value** is the probability of seeing a difference at least that large if the true difference were zero. Small p means the sample would be surprising under no effect. The book uses these words as reported by the source, and never as evidence on their own.  A **null model** is a version of the data with the structure under test removed and everything else kept. The right comparison for a measured statistic is the same statistic on the null, not zero. Chapter 8 section 8.4 has three cases where the null was more than half the apparent effect.  *Example.* Suppose 1000 queries each retrieve 10 neighbours from 100000 points, so 10000 slots are handed out and the average point gets 0.1. If slots were handed out at random, the count for one point would follow a Poisson distribution with mean 0.1, under which a count of 4 or more has probability about 4 in a million. Among 100000 points, about 0.4 of them would reach 4 by chance, so the no-structure ceiling is 3 and a point with count 30 is ten times the ceiling. Chapter 3 section 3.5 uses exactly this null.  $$\Pr[X\ge c]=1-\sum_{i<c}e^{-\mu}\frac{\mu^{i}}{i!},\qquad c^{\star}=\max\{c:\ n\Pr[X\ge c]\ge 1\},\qquad \mu=\frac{n_q\,k}{n}. \tag{0.17}$$  **Multiple comparisons.** Test forty hypotheses at a five percent threshold and two will pass by chance. Reporting the best of forty as if it were the only test overstates it forty-fold. TSK chapter 10 covers the standard corrections. Chapter 8 section 8.5 adds the case of repeated cross-validation, where the folds are not independent and a standard test counts each one as if it were [@nadeau2003].  $$\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{NB}}=\Big(\frac1J+\frac{n_{\mathrm{test}}}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\Big)\hat\sigma^{2},\qquad \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{\mathrm{NB}}}{\hat\sigma^{2}/J}=1+J\,\frac{n_{\mathrm{test}}}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}. \tag{0.18}$$  *Used in.* Chapter 8 throughout. Chapter 3 section 3.5.  ## 0.10 Graphs and their spectra  A **graph** is a set of nodes and a set of edges joining pairs of them. The **degree** of a node is the number of edges it has. A **neighbourhood graph** on a dataset joins each row to its k nearest rows, or to every row within a radius. The **geodesic distance** between two nodes is the length of the shortest path along edges. On a neighbourhood graph built from points on a curved surface, geodesic distance follows the surface rather than cutting through the space around it.  A **manifold** is a curved surface of some dimension, like a sphere or a torus, that looks flat when viewed closely. A dataset lies on a manifold when its rows, though given in many coordinates, really vary along only a few. The number of directions it varies along is the **intrinsic dimension**. A sheet rolled into a spiral in three dimensions has intrinsic dimension two.  The **Laplacian** of a graph is a matrix built from its edges. In the normalized form the book uses, its entry for a pair of joined nodes is negative one over the square root of the product of their degrees, its diagonal is one, and everything else is zero. Its eigenvectors, ordered by eigenvalue from smallest up, are the smoothest functions on the graph. The first is constant. The next few vary slowly from one region of the graph to another. Using a node's values in the first few nontrivial eigenvectors as its coordinates is the **spectral embedding**, and clustering those coordinates is spectral clustering, which TSK covers in chapter 7 [@tan2019; @ng2002].  $$L=I-D^{-1/2}AD^{-1/2},\qquad D=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\dots,d_n),\qquad L\,u_k=\lambda_k u_k,\ \ 0=\lambda_1\le\lambda_2\le\cdots. \tag{0.19}$$  Two facts about that embedding matter for chapter 3. First, as a neighbourhood graph on a manifold grows, its low eigenvectors converge to the smoothest functions on the manifold itself, so the embedding becomes a picture of the manifold. Second, a particular scaling of the embedding, dividing each eigenvector by the square root of its eigenvalue, makes Euclidean distance in the embedding equal the **commute time**, the expected number of steps a random walk needs to go from one node to the other and back. Chapter 3 section 3.3 is about what happens to that distance on large graphs.  $$\Psi_i=\left(\frac{u_k(i)}{\sqrt{\lambda_k\,d_i}}\right)_{k\ge2},\qquad \|\Psi_i-\Psi_j\|^{2}=R(i,j)=\frac{C(i,j)}{\operatorname{vol}(G)}. \tag{0.20}$$  A map between two spaces is **bi-Lipschitz** when it neither stretches nor shrinks any distance by more than a fixed factor. If a map is bi-Lipschitz with factor 2, every pair of points ends up between half and twice as far apart as it started. Chapter 3 uses the word for embeddings that preserve geodesic distance up to such a factor. The weaker property that the ordering of distances is preserved, without any bound on the stretch, is called **rank-faithful**.  $$\frac1K\,d(x,y)\ \le\ d'\big(f(x),f(y)\big)\ \le\ K\,d(x,y)\qquad\text{for all }x,y. \tag{0.21}$$  *Example.* A path graph on nodes 1 through 5, each joined to its neighbours. Degrees are 1, 2, 2, 2, 1. The geodesic distance from node 1 to node 5 is 4. The second eigenvector of the Laplacian, once each node's value is divided by the square root of its degree, rises monotonically from one end to the other, so plotting nodes by that one coordinate recovers the line. Without the degree correction the two end nodes, which have degree 1 where the others have degree 2, sit slightly out of order. That correction is the row normalization chapter 3 is about, seen for the first time.  ![Figure 0.3](../figures/ch00_fig3.svg)  *Figure 0.3. The second eigenvector of a ten-node path graph's normalized Laplacian, as computed and after dividing each node's value by the square root of its degree. Only the corrected one is in order at the two ends.*  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.3, chapter 9.  ## 0.11 The language-model vocabulary the examples use  Several of the book's measurements were made on language models, because they are the largest consumers of vectors in current practice. The reader does not need to know how those models work. The following words are enough.  A **token** is a piece of text, roughly a word. A language model reads a sequence of tokens and outputs a probability for the next one. **Perplexity** is a score for that output on a test text, equal to two to the power of the average number of bits the model needs per token. Lower is better. A perplexity of 12 means the model is about as uncertain as a fair choice among 12 tokens. A perplexity of 10000 means the model has been broken.  $$\mathrm{PPL}=2^{H},\qquad H=-\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log_2 p\big(w_t\mid w_{<t}\big). \tag{0.22}$$  Inside the model, **attention** is the operation that lets each token look at earlier ones. Each token produces three vectors, a **query**, a **key**, and a **value**. The query is compared to every earlier key by dot product, the dot products are turned into weights by the **softmax** function, which exponentiates them and divides by their sum so they are positive and add to one, and the weights are used to average the values. A **head** is one such operation. A model has many, and each has its own queries, keys, and values. The **KV cache** is the stored keys and values of earlier tokens, kept so they need not be recomputed. Compressing it is the subject of several examples, and the keys are the case where compression goes wrong, because the softmax reads the dot product with the query, which depends on each key's coordinates at their own scale.  $$\operatorname{softmax}(z)_i=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}},\qquad \text{output}=\sum_i\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{q\cdot k_i}{\sqrt{d}}\Big)_{\!i}\,v_i. \tag{0.23}$$  The **rotary position embedding** is a rotation applied to queries and keys that encodes where each token sits in the sequence. The book mentions it only to say that the keys were measured after it was applied. **Teacher forcing** means feeding the model the correct text as context rather than its own earlier outputs. It removes the model's own mistakes from the context and is used in chapter 11 to test whether a failure compounds.  **Quantization** replaces each number with one of a small set of allowed values, called a **codebook**, so that it can be stored in a few bits. A **per-channel** quantizer chooses a scale per coordinate. A **direction-only** quantizer stores each vector's length exactly and rounds its direction. **Product quantization** splits a vector into pieces and quantizes each piece with its own codebook. An **inverted file** index partitions the vectors into cells by k-means, which TSK covers in chapter 7, and answers a query by searching only the cells whose centres are nearest to it. The number of cells searched is the probe count, and **recall at k** is the fraction of a query's true k nearest neighbours that the index returned.  **KL divergence** measures how far one probability distribution is from another, in units called nats when natural logarithms are used. Zero means identical. The book quotes it once, in chapter 4, as a distortion between two attention outputs. **ROUGE** is a family of scores for generated text against a reference. The book quotes one variant, ROUGE-L, in chapter 8, as the metric a retracted result was measured in.  $$\mathrm{KL}(p\,\|\,q)=\sum_i p_i\ln\frac{p_i}{q_i}\ \ge 0. \tag{0.24}$$  *Used in.* Chapter 4 section 4.2, chapter 8 section 8.9, chapter 11 sections 11.2 and 11.9.  ## 0.12 Hashes, commits, and seals  A **hash** is a short fixed-length fingerprint of a file computed so that any change to the file changes the fingerprint. SHA-256 is the standard hash the book's sources use. A **commit hash** is the fingerprint git assigns to a snapshot of a repository, and it fixes the time at which a file existed in that state. When the book says a prediction was **sealed**, it means the file stating the prediction was committed, its hash recorded, and the commit made before the measurement was run. Anyone with the repository can verify that the sealed file has not changed since.  *Used in.* Chapter 8 sections 8.8 and 8.9, and every sources table.  ## 0.13 Time, staleness, and the cost of a bit  Everything above is static. Chapter 13 adds a clock, and needs six more words.  A quantity that changes over time has a **coherence time**, the interval over which its value stays correlated with itself. A radio channel between a moving phone and a tower has a coherence time set by the phone's speed. Clarke's formula gives it as 0.423 divided by the Doppler frequency [@clarke1968], so a 10 hertz Doppler gives about 42 milliseconds and 400 hertz gives about 1 millisecond. A value measured longer ago than the coherence time is a guess.  $$T_{\mathrm{coh}}=\frac{0.423}{f_D}. \tag{0.25}$$  A **certificate** is a claim, made at a time, that something is safe to act on. A database replica is fresh enough. A network route has settled. A radio channel estimate is still valid. A **witness** is an independent measurement of whether the claim was true. The replica's write-ahead position, the packets that actually arrived, the acknowledgement or negative acknowledgement the receiver sends back. The **false-clear rate** is the fraction of the decisions the certificate cleared in which the witness said otherwise. It is conditional on clearing, and it is reported beside the certificate's **coverage**, the fraction of decisions it clears, because a certificate that clears nothing has a false-clear rate of zero and no use. The joint rate, cleared and wrong over all decisions, is the product of the two, and the book names it as the joint rate whenever a source reports that form. A certificate whose false-clear rate exceeds the error it promised to hold is worthless for that consumer no matter how confident it looks.  $$\mathrm{FC}=\Pr\big[W\ \text{refutes}\ \big|\ \mathcal C_t\ \text{clears}\big],\qquad \text{coverage}=\Pr\big[\mathcal C_t\ \text{clears}\big].

Each scored between 0.75 and 0.955 on held-out rows of its own corpus. Scored on a second, independent corpus of the same dimension, every one collapsed to between 0.47 and 0.55, which is chance. The within-corpus numbers were artefacts of the corpus's surface, because learning the surface is the easiest way to separate a corpus's pairs.  **The fix.** Train each dimension on at least two corpora at once, drawing every same-sign positive from a different corpus than its anchor. To pull a text from corpus A next to a same-valence text from corpus B, the encoder cannot use A's surface. Held-out AUROC is then cross-corpus by construction. A gradient-reversal domain head was available as an extra control and the record says the load-bearing fix was the cross-corpus positives, not the adversary, which only helps when the two corpora are surface-similar.  **The gate.** A dimension is validated when its cross-corpus AUROC beats both of two nulls by at least 0.10 on the same held-out pairs. The untrained encoder, and a TF-IDF bag-of-words model. The second null is the section 12.1 reader used as a control. If the counts can separate the pairs, the encoder has proved nothing the counts did not know.  $$\text{validated}\iff \mathrm{AUROC}_{\text{cross}}-\max\big(\mathrm{AUROC}_{\text{untrained}},\ \mathrm{AUROC}_{\text{BoW}}\big)\ \ge\ 0.10. \tag{12.3}$$  **The scorecard.** Eight of nine dimensions passed, with cross-corpus AUROC from 0.622 to 0.853 and bag-of-words between 0.46 and 0.54 on every one, so the encoders' signal is not lexical. It encodes surface smoothly, so an age of 17 sits beside 19 and a price of 999 beside 1001, and it cannot see a stratum it never learned, such as the legal threshold that separates them. A score from an unvalidated encoder cannot enter a decision, because the validation record is bound into the decision's audit artifact. ## conditions

- A witness must exist. Where none exists the rate is not measurable and the certificate is unassessed, not safe.
- The rate is per consumer, given per read operator, slice, group, or footprint, never as one aggregate.
- Coverage is reported with it. A witnessed certificate clears fewer decisions than a naive one, and the two numbers are read together.

Conditions are curated in `entries.toml` rather than read from a record.
