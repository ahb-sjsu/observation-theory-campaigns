Lost to whom.  ## 4.2 Bits go where the consumer is sensitive, not where the variance is  Fix a bit budget and a set of directions. Each direction has a source variance, how much the data spreads along it, and a sensitivity, how much the consumer's output moves when that direction is perturbed. The distortion the consumer feels is, for a uniform quantizer at high rate in the covariance's own eigenbasis, the sum over directions of sensitivity times variance times a factor that falls by four for each bit spent on that direction, since a bit halves the rounding error and quarters its square. Both the variance $\sigma_i^2$ and the sensitivity $s_i$ are read in the covariance's eigenbasis $v_i$, the sensitivity along the $i$th eigenvector being $v_i^{\top}P_C v_i$, so that the two numbers describe the same direction. The eigenvalues of the read operator and of the covariance can be paired only when the two share an eigenbasis, and the formula does not assume they do. That is the approximation of chapter 0 section 0.7, and the closed form below is a theorem only under those two conditions. Minimizing that sum under a fixed total number of bits gives each direction half the base-two logarithm of its sensitivity-weighted variance over a water level, or zero if that quantity is below the water. Directions the consumer does not read, or the data does not populate, get no bits at all. Section 0.7 works a three-direction example by hand.  $$D(b)=\sum_i s_i\sigma_i^{2}\,2^{-2b_i},\qquad s_i=v_i^{\top}P_C\,v_i,\qquad b_i^{\star}=\max\!\Big(0,\ \tfrac12\log_2\frac{s_i\sigma_i^{2}}{\theta}\Big),\qquad \sum_i b_i^{\star}=B, \tag{4.2}$$  ![Figure 4.1](../figures/ch04_fig1.svg)  *Figure 4.1. A d by d matrix A sends x to Ax. The matrices in this book are almost all symmetric, meaning the entry in row i and column j equals the entry in row j and column i.  A projection onto a direction u of length one sends x to the multiple of u that is closest to x. That multiple is the dot product of x with u, times u. The number itself, the dot product with u, is the coordinate of x along u.  $$\operatorname{proj}_u(x)=(u\cdot x)\,u,\qquad \|u\|=1. \tag{0.2}$$  A subspace is a set of directions closed under addition and scaling. The set of all vectors along u is a one-dimensional subspace. The set of all vectors in a plane through the origin is a two-dimensional subspace. The rank of a matrix is the dimension of the subspace its outputs fill. A rank-one matrix sends every input to a multiple of one fixed direction.  *Example.* Let u = (0.6, 0.8), which has length one. Projecting x = (3, 4) onto u gives coordinate 1.8 + 3.2 = 5, so the projection is 5u = (3, 4). Projecting x = (4, 3) onto u gives 2.4 + 2.4 = 4.8, so the projection is (2.88, 3.84).  *Used in.* Section 0.5, where a read operator is a weighted sum of projections. Chapters 3, 4, and 11.  ## 0.3 Variance in many dimensions  For one column, the variance is the average squared distance from the mean. For d columns, the covariance matrix is the d by d table whose diagonal entries are each column's variance and whose off-diagonal entries measure how two columns move together. It is symmetric. Write it as $\Sigma$.  The trace of a square matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. The trace of a covariance matrix is the total variance across all columns. Two datasets can have the same total variance and distribute it across columns completely differently.  $$\Sigma_{ij}=\mathbb E\big[(x_i-\mu_i)(x_j-\mu_j)\big],\qquad \operatorname{tr}\Sigma=\sum_{i}\Sigma_{ii}. \tag{0.3}$$  *Example.* A dataset with variance 0.3 in the first coordinate and 1.7 in the second, and no covariance between them, has covariance matrix with diagonal (0.3, 1.7) and trace 2.0. A dataset with the diagonal reversed, (1.7, 0.3), also has trace 2.0. Chapter 3 shows a reader that ranks these two 4.1 to one.  The variance along a direction $u$ of length one is $u^{\top}\Sigma u$, the dot product of $u$ with $\Sigma u$. It is the variance of the coordinates along u. For the first dataset and u = (1, 0) it is 0.3. For u at 15 degrees from the first axis, u = (0.966, 0.259), it is 0.966 squared times 0.3 plus 0.259 squared times 1.7, which is 0.280 + 0.114 = 0.394.  $$\operatorname{Var}(u\cdot x)=u^{\top}\Sigma\,u. \tag{0.4}$$  ![Figure 0.1](../figures/ch00_fig1.svg)  *Figure 0.1. Two covariances with the same trace, 2.0, and a reader at 15 degrees. The variance the reader sees is 0.394 along the first and 1.606 along the second.*  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.2 and chapter 4 throughout. Whenever the book writes the trace of one matrix times another, it means a weighted total variance, with the weights given by the first matrix.  ## 0.4 Eigenvalues, eigenvectors, and the spectrum  A symmetric d by d matrix has d special directions, its eigenvectors, along which it acts as pure scaling. Along eigenvector v with eigenvalue lambda, the matrix sends v to lambda v. The eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix can be chosen orthogonal to each other, and the eigenvalues of a covariance matrix are never negative. The list of eigenvalues is called the spectrum.  $$\Sigma\,v_i=\lambda_i v_i,\qquad \Sigma=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_i\,v_i v_i^{\top},\qquad v_i\cdot v_j=0\ (i\ne j). \tag{0.5}$$  For a covariance matrix, the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the direction of largest variance, and the eigenvalue is that variance. That is principal component analysis. TSK Appendix B derives it [@tan2019]. The book uses the vocabulary as follows.  **Isotropic** means every direction has the same variance. All eigenvalues are equal. **Anisotropic** means they differ. A spectrum is **concentrated** when a few eigenvalues carry most of the total.  **Whitening** rescales the data so that its covariance becomes the identity matrix. It divides each eigenvector direction by the square root of its eigenvalue. After whitening every direction has variance one, and distances no longer favour the high-variance directions.  $$x_{\mathrm w}=\Sigma^{-1/2}(x-\mu),\qquad \Sigma^{-1/2}=\sum_i\lambda_i^{-1/2}\,v_i v_i^{\top}. \tag{0.6}$$  **Effective rank** answers the question of how many directions a matrix really uses, without choosing a cutoff. It is the square of the sum of the eigenvalues divided by the sum of the squared eigenvalues. It equals one when a single eigenvalue carries everything and equals d when all eigenvalues are equal.  $$r_{\mathrm{eff}}=\frac{\big(\sum_i\lambda_i\big)^{2}}{\sum_i\lambda_i^{2}}. \tag{0.7}$$  *Example.* Eigenvalues (4, 1, 0, 0). Sum is 5, sum of squares is 17, effective rank is 25 over 17, or 1.47. The matrix mostly uses one direction and partly a second. Eigenvalues (1, 1, 1, 1) give effective rank 16 over 4, or 4.  *Used in.* Chapter 3 section 3.3, where the eigenvectors of a graph's Laplacian give the spectral embedding. Chapter 4 sections 4.2 and 4.5. Chapter 11 section 11.7.  ## 0.5 Reading a vector, and the read operator  A consumer is any computation that takes a vector in and gives a number out. A classifier's score for one class. A ranking function's score for one candidate. A regression's prediction. Call the number C(x).  The **sensitivity** of the consumer at x is the vector of partial derivatives of C with respect to each coordinate. It is also called the gradient and written g. It points in the direction in which a small change to x moves the output most. A coordinate the consumer never uses has a zero entry in g.  Sensitivity can be measured without knowing the formula for C. Change coordinate i of x by a small amount h, evaluate C again, and divide the change in output by h. That is a **finite difference**, and doing it once per coordinate costs d evaluations of C. Doing it in both directions, plus and minus h, costs 2d and is more accurate. Chapter 11 section 11.7 says why 2d is the price at one operating point for a probe confined to the directions it chooses, and that whether many cheaper points can average their way back to the population operator was measured within a budget range, not proved.  $$g_j\;\approx\;\frac{C(x+h\,e_j)-C(x-h\,e_j)}{2h},\qquad j=1,\dots,d. \tag{0.8}$$  The **read operator** of a consumer summarizes its sensitivity over a whole dataset. For each row, form the $d$ by $d$ matrix whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $g_i g_j$. This is the outer product of $g$ with itself. Average those matrices over the rows. The result, written $P_C$, is symmetric, its eigenvectors are the directions the consumer is most sensitive to on average, and its eigenvalues say how sensitive. Directions the consumer never reads have eigenvalue zero. The eigenvectors with nonzero eigenvalue span the **read subspace**. $P_C$ is an average of local, linearized sensitivities. Each local operator is positive semidefinite, so squared sensitivities cannot cancel in the average, and the kernel of the average is the intersection of the local kernels over the rows, up to sets of rows of measure zero. A direction with eigenvalue zero in $\bar P_{C,\mu}$ is therefore locally unread at almost every row of the workload, for a nonlinear consumer as much as for a linear one. What the average does not give is a global statement about pairs of inputs, and section 0.6 says what that costs. Chapter 2 section 2.2 keeps the pointwise operator, equation 0.11 evaluated at one row, apart from this average, and the book says which it means wherever the difference matters. The average is also taken over a particular set of rows, a dataset or a workload, and depends on which. Where that matters the book writes $\bar P_{C,\mu}$ with $\mu$ the distribution the rows were drawn from, and a read operator measured on one footprint is a claim about that footprint.  $$P_C=\mathbb E\!\left[g\,g^{\top}\right],\qquad g=\nabla C(x). \tag{0.9}$$  The book's central quantity is the trace of $P_C$ times $M_\delta$, where $M_\delta$ is the second-moment matrix of an error, the average over rows of the error's outer product with itself. The identity below is exact, with no centering, for a fixed read operator, which is the linear case. For a nonlinear consumer whose error depends on the row, the first-order distortion is the average over rows of $\delta(x)^{\top}P_C(x)\delta(x)$, and the trace of the averaged operator times the averaged second moment is a factorized surrogate of it, exact when the operator and the error's outer product are uncorrelated across rows and otherwise an approximation, which the book uses because it is what the instruments of chapter 11 can measure. When the error has mean zero, $M_\delta$ is also the error's covariance, written $\Sigma_\delta$, and the book writes $\Sigma_\delta$ only where an error is centered by construction. A reconstruction error is not centered merely to remove its bias, since the consumer feels the bias too, and a reader who wants the covariance form must add the mean's own term, $\mu_\delta^{\top}P_C\mu_\delta$. The trace of $M_\delta$ is the mean squared vector norm of the error, which is $d$ times the per-coordinate mean squared error a report usually quotes. By section 0.3, that is the total variance of the error weighted by how much the consumer reads each direction. It is the error as the consumer experiences it, to first order and in the factorized form the next paragraph qualifies. Setting P_C to the identity matrix weights every direction equally and recovers plain total squared error. The book calls that the **identity reader**.  $$d_O=\operatorname{tr}(P_C\,M_\delta)=\mathbb E\!\left[\delta^{\top}P_C\,\delta\right],\qquad M_\delta=\mathbb E\!\left[\delta\delta^{\top}\right],\qquad P_C=I\ \Rightarrow\ d_O=\operatorname{tr}M_\delta.

## conditions

- Matched bits, and reconstruction matched or reported, for every comparison of two codes.
- A read operator misaligned with where the signal has its energy. The alignment of book equation 4.6 is the dial, and near one, the coupling null, no flip is possible.
- The read operator is the average of local linearizations, and the ordering it predicts is the ordering on average over the workload.
