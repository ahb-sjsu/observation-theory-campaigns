\title{The Optical Margin is a Consumer-Relative Certificate: Measuring the
Quality-of-Transmission False-Clear Rate Under Incremental Fill}

\author{Andrew H. Bond$^{*}$}
\address{Department of Computer Engineering, San Jos\'e State University, San Jos\'e, CA 95192, USA}
\email{$^{*}$andrew.bond@sjsu.edu}

\begin{abstract}
As a link fills, in-service lightpaths certified under light loading false-clear
their FEC threshold---consumer-relatively, by footprint. We measure this
Quality-of-Transmission false-clear rate on GNPy over CORONET-CONUS and recover
margin with a footprint-witnessed certificate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Optical networks grow \emph{incrementally}. An operator lights a new 400G/600G
service on a partially filled C-band link, and the pre-transmission
Quality-of-Transmission (QoT) tool certifies a modulation format against the current
nonlinear-interference (NLI) environment: the estimated generalized signal-to-noise
ratio (GSNR) clears the format's forward-error-correction (FEC) threshold with
margin, so the service is provisioned. Over the following months the operator sells
more capacity and the band fills. Each added channel raises the NLI seen by the
\emph{already-deployed} lightpaths; a service that was certified under light fill can
drift below its FEC threshold and take errors---an outage on an in-service wavelength
that ``passed QoT.'' Operators forestall this with system margin, i.e.\ capacity
deliberately left on the table~\cite{pointon}; reducing that margin via monitoring
and machine-learning QoT (ML-QoT) is among the largest available capacity
levers~\cite{mlqot}.

This brownfield-growth scenario exposes what the margin really is. The QoT
certificate is issued for a specific lightpath, but the risk it hides is set by the
\emph{future neighbours that particular lightpath will see}---its \emph{footprint}
(spectral position and reach). The margin is therefore \emph{consumer-relative}: a
single blanket value is wasteful for a lightpath in a lightly loaded region and
unsafe for one whose footprint will fill densely. We make the failure measurable via
the \emph{false-clear rate}---the fraction of provisioned lightpaths whose witnessed
GSNR (the coherent receiver's pre-FEC BER) later violates the certificate that
provisioned them---and show (i) it is a footprint property, with a refresh law for
re-certification, and (ii) an ML-QoT estimator trained on \emph{average} error
inherits the same blindness and false-clears at the FEC cliff.

\section{Method}
\textbf{Network, link, and fill.} Consumers are lightpaths over the
\textbf{CORONET-CONUS} reference topology (75 nodes): each is a transparent
shortest-path city-pair route ($\le 2500$\,km, 2--31 $\times$ 80\,km spans; median
$\approx 30$ spans). Per-channel GSNR is computed with the GN model in
GNPy~\cite{gnpy}, the community-standard QoT engine, on standard single-mode fibre
(0.2\,dB/km, $D=17$\,ps/nm/km) with EDFAs (NF 6\,dB), +2\,dBm/channel, 32\,GBd on a
50\,GHz grid. The \emph{provisioning-time} loading is a lightly filled band (6
channels); the \emph{deployed} loading is the fully filled C-band (76 channels).

\textbf{Certificate, consumer, witness.} A \emph{consumer} is a lightpath
$(\text{position},\text{reach})$. Its \emph{certificate} is the format selected from
the GSNR under provisioning-time loading (1\,dB design margin, 0.3\,dB monitoring
noise); required GSNR per format follows a standard table (QPSK\,6.5, 8QAM\,9.0,
16QAM\,12.5, 32QAM\,16.0, 64QAM\,19.0\,dB). Its \emph{witness} is the true GSNR under
deployed (full) loading. A \emph{naive} operator trusts the provisioning-time
certificate; a \emph{footprint-aware} operator certifies against the loading the
lightpath will actually see. A certificate \emph{false-clears} when it clears a
format whose required GSNR exceeds the witnessed GSNR. Bars, cooling-off, and
manipulation checks are pre-registered; the sealed record is content-hashed and the
graded seeds are disjoint from calibration~\cite{otcampaigns}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{The margin is a footprint property}
Of the lightpaths provisioned under light fill, the naive certificate false-clears
$\mathrm{FC}_{\text{naive}}\approx 0.5$ % GRADED-SWAP (0.48--0.55 across CORONET routes)
once the band fills (mean fill-induced GSNR penalty $\approx 2.6$\,dB), while the
footprint-aware certificate holds at $\mathrm{FC}_{\text{aware}}\approx 0$. % GRADED-SWAP
The outages are \emph{footprint-concentrated} (per-lightpath false-clear spread
$\approx 0.49$): % GRADED-SWAP
they fall on the services whose spectral position and reach place their GSNR nearest
a format threshold, and are absent elsewhere (Fig.~\ref{fig:fc}). No blanket margin
is therefore simultaneously safe for the threshold-adjacent services and efficient
for the rest; the safe margin is per-footprint, and the false-clear rate quantifies
it---directly, the capacity recoverable by certifying each service against its own
fill trajectory rather than a worst-case constant.

\subsection{When a certificate must be refreshed}
Because the false-clear is driven by the fill \emph{increment} between provisioning
and use, a QoT certificate has a coherence horizon in fill. The maximum added-channel
count a certificate tolerates at a target false-clear rate scales with that horizon
(slope $\approx K$, $R^2\approx r$); % GRADED-SWAP (from the fill sweep)
the operational reading is a refresh rule---re-certify a service \emph{before} the
band around it fills, not on a fixed calendar~\cite{otcampaigns}.

\subsection{ML-QoT trained on the wrong metric}
The same blindness appears in ML-QoT. Predicting deployed GSNR from provisioning-time
features carries irreducible uncertainty (unmeasured state); an estimator trained to
minimise \emph{average} error optimises reconstruction, but the consumer reads a
\emph{threshold}. On the GNPy data, the average-error estimator attains lower mean
error yet false-clears far more at the FEC cliff than a consumer-aware
(conservative-quantile) objective---MAE $0.65$ vs $0.95$\,dB (it wins reconstruction)
but false-clear $\approx 0.04$ vs $\approx 0.005$ ($\sim$8$\times$) at comparable
delivered spectral efficiency ($\approx 3.9$\,bits/symbol) (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml}). The
implication for QoT learning is to train on the consumer-read decision near the
threshold, not on volume-averaged fidelity.

\section{Related work}
Physical-layer-aware routing/spectrum assignment and QoT estimation are
mature~\cite{gnpy,pointon}; ML-QoT and margin reduction are active, with estimators
trained largely on prediction error~\cite{mlqot}. Our contribution is not another
estimator or allocator but a \emph{measurement}: the false-clear rate as a
first-class, consumer-relative KPI, the finding that QoT margin under incremental
fill is a footprint property, and the corollary that the ML-QoT \emph{objective}
should target the consumer's threshold.

\section{Discussion}
Margin is usually reported in dB and applied uniformly; under incremental fill it
should be reported as a false-clear rate and allocated per footprint. The GN model is
the evidence rung; a coherent-receiver pre-FEC-BER witness on a fibre testbed, and the
EGN/split-step model, are the external-validity graduations. The method is
domain-general---the same certificate/witness/false-clear grammar has been measured
across routing, distributed databases, and wireless physical layers~\cite{otcampaigns},
of which optical QoT is one instance.
