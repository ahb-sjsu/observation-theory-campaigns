\title{One CSI Report Cannot Serve Every Consumer:\\
The Consumer-Relative False-Clear Rate in 5G/6G Link Adaptation}

\begin{abstract}
A CSI report carries a promise: the channel will support the rate selected from it.
Whether the promise holds depends on who relies on it. We measure how often it fails,
on a 5G NR link with real LDPC decoding, and show that the failure rate depends on the
reader in two ways. First, the reliability target. An MCS choice that meets an eMBB
error target of $10^{-1}$ misses a URLLC target of $10^{-3}$ on the same channel by a
factor of 112, and it does so at every SNR from cell edge to cell centre. A policy that
knows the stricter target meets it. Second, time. As a report ages the failure rate
grows, and the longest safe report period is about $0.177$ of the Clarke coherence time
($R^2=0.915$), until at high mobility no period the frame permits is short enough. The
corrections are standard, outer-loop link adaptation and faster reporting. What we add
is the measured failure rate that says how much each correction is worth and when it
stops being optional. A third experiment shows the verdict itself can invert. With
equal margin budgets, one user fleet does better under one policy and the other fleet
under the other, so no fleet-level number serves both. Every number comes from a
sealed, pre-registered record.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
CSI feedback, link adaptation, URLLC, outer-loop link adaptation, reliability, 5G NR.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Adaptive modulation and coding turns a CSI report into a rate decision. The transmitter
selects the highest MCS whose required SINR the report clears. The report therefore
carries an implicit claim: the link will decode at the chosen rate. We call the
optimistic failure of that claim a false-clear. The CSI cleared an MCS the channel
cannot support, and the block is lost.

Whether a report false-clears is not decided by the channel alone. It is decided by the
report together with whoever acts on it. Two schedulers can read the same CSI and
disagree, because they hold traffic to different requirements. This paper measures that
disagreement on a real 5G NR link.

The disagreement shows up along two axes. The first is the reliability target
(\S\ref{sec:urllc}). An MCS that serves eMBB adequately misses a URLLC target on the
same channel by more than a hundred times, and the gap persists at every SNR we tested.
The second is time (\S\ref{sec:refresh}). As the report ages the false-clear rate
grows, and the longest safe report period is a fixed fraction of the channel coherence
time, until mobility is high enough that no period is short enough.

Neither observation needs a new estimator or scheduler. The corrections are old.
Outer-loop link adaptation \cite{olla} closes the target gap, and faster reporting
closes the age gap. What has been missing is the measured false-clear rate itself,
which says how large the exposure is when a correction is absent, and when a
correction has stopped being an optimization and become a requirement.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Substrate.} We use the 5G NR LDPC decoder in Sionna \cite{sionna} as ground
truth. For each MCS we measure a block-error curve $e_m(\gamma)$ by decoding 400 coded
blocks per SNR point on a grid from $-8$ to $28$\,dB in $2$\,dB steps, over AWGN. The
link then draws a per-TTI SNR trace from a 3GPP TR38.901 TDL-A channel at $3.5$\,GHz
with $30$\,ns delay spread, and at each TTI the HARQ outcome of the selected MCS is
drawn from $e_m$ at the true SNR. This is a standard link-to-system mapping, built on
measured decoder curves rather than a formula. The MCS set is the NR table (TS~38.214
Table~5.1.3.1-1), QPSK through 64-QAM at code rates of $1/5$ and up.
Table~\ref{tab:params} lists the parameters.

\textbf{System model.} Let $\gamma(t)$ be the true SNR in dB at TTI $t$ and
$\hat{g}(t)$ the estimate the transmitter holds. Fresh CSI reads
$\hat{g}(t)=\gamma(t)+n(t)$ with $n\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^2)$ and $\sigma=1$\,dB. A
report held with period $P$ reads $\hat{g}(t)=\gamma\!\left(P\lfloor t/P\rfloor\right)$
and ages between reports. Let $\rho_m(\beta)=\min\{\gamma:e_m(\gamma)\le\beta\}$ be the
SNR that MCS $m$ needs to meet target $\beta$. The certificate selects, at a fixed
reference target $\beta_0=10^{-1}$ with backoff $\Delta$,
\begin{equation}
m^\star(\hat{g})=\max\{m\in\mathcal{M}:\rho_m(\beta_0)+\Delta\le\hat{g}\},\quad
\Delta{=}0\ \text{(naive)}.
\label{eq:cert}
\end{equation}
The operating point is set by $\beta_0$. The outcome is judged against the consumer's
own target $\beta$, which may differ. The witness is the HARQ outcome,
$\mathrm{NACK}(t)\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(e_{m^\star}(\gamma(t)))$. A false-clear is a
cleared MCS the channel cannot support at $\beta$, that is
$\rho_{m^\star}(\beta)>\gamma(t)$. We report its rate with two estimators. The refresh
study (\S\ref{sec:refresh}) counts actual NACKs,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{FC}}=\tfrac{1}{T}\textstyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbf{1}[\mathrm{NACK}(t)],
\label{eq:fc_emp}
\end{equation}
which is also what the outer loop consumes. The target study (\S\ref{sec:urllc}) uses
the expected rate $\mathrm{FC}(\beta)=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t e_{m^\star}(\gamma(t))$,
because $T=6000$ TTI cannot resolve a $10^{-3}$ tail by counting. Since \eqref{eq:cert}
fixes the operating point at $\beta_0$ while the verdict uses $\beta$, one trace gives
different answers to the eMBB consumer ($\beta{=}10^{-1}$) and the URLLC consumer
($\beta{=}10^{-3}$). That difference is what we measure.

\textbf{URLLC-aware policy.} It raises $\Delta$ and combines $K{=}3$ diversity
branches. A backoff alone cannot reach $10^{-3}$, because a single Rayleigh branch
spends about $4\%$ of its time in fades too deep for any MCS. The branches
$\{\gamma_k(t)\}$ are independent TDL-A realizations from distinct seeds, standing in
for frequency or spatial replicas rather than retransmissions, and a block fails only
if all branches fail:
$\mathrm{FC}_{\text{aware}}=\tfrac{1}{T}\sum_t\prod_{k=1}^{K}e_{m^\star}(\gamma_k(t))$.
Independence makes this a best case. Correlated diversity fails together more often, so
the true cost of URLLC safety is at least what we report.

\textbf{OLLA.} The correction of \S\ref{sec:refresh} does not trust $\hat{g}$. It
selects $m^\star(\hat{g}(t)-o(t))$ and moves the offset on HARQ feedback,
\begin{equation}
o(t{+}1)=o(t)+\delta_\uparrow\mathbf{1}[\text{ACK}(t)]
-\delta_\downarrow\mathbf{1}[\text{NACK}(t)],
\label{eq:olla}
\end{equation}
with $\delta_\uparrow=0.1$\,dB and $\delta_\downarrow=\delta_\uparrow(1-\beta)/\beta$,
so the loop settles where the NACK rate equals $\beta$. For held CSI the refresh floor
is $P^\star(f_D)=\max\{P:\widehat{\mathrm{FC}}\le\beta\}$, the longest report period
that still meets the target.

\textbf{Discipline.} Each experiment is pre-registered. Numerical bars and manipulation
checks are fixed before the graded run, the sealed record is content-hashed, grading
uses seeds disjoint from those used during design, and failed or voided outcomes stay
in the record \cite{otcampaigns}. The numbers below are the graded values
(Table~\ref{tab:results}).

\section{The Reliability Target}
\label{sec:urllc}
A base station serving mixed traffic derives one CSI estimate per user and picks an MCS
from it. Consider the same estimate read under two targets, $10^{-1}$ for eMBB and
$10^{-3}$ for URLLC. We apply the naive CSI-derived MCS on a real NR link and measure
the achieved BLER against each target. Then we compare a target-aware policy that sizes
its protection to the stricter target.

Figure~\ref{fig:urllc} shows the sealed result, three graded seeds of $6000$ TTI each
with fresh CSI. The naive certificate lands at a BLER near $0.112$. The eMBB consumer
reads this as sound, since it sits at the target the MCS was chosen for. The URLLC
consumer reads the same $0.112$ as a failure 112 times over budget. Each of those
blocks breaks a reliability agreement, and nothing in the scheduler's view flags it,
because the report looked fine. The target-aware policy uses the same CSI with a
conservative MCS and the three independent branches of \S\ref{sec:method}, and reaches
$1.2\times10^{-5}$, within target. It pays in spectral efficiency and in diversity
resources. So neither certificate serves both consumers well. The one sound for eMBB
fails URLLC by $112\times$, and the one sound for URLLC over-provisions eMBB. The
false-clear rate prices the difference.

The gap is not an artifact of the operating point we happened to pick. A separately
sealed cell sweeps the mean SNR from $3$ to $21$\,dB (Fig.~\ref{fig:snr}). The
certificate always selects the MCS that rides the eMBB target, so the URLLC consumer
reads a false-clear of $0.08$ to $0.14$, between 77 and 142 times its budget, at every
SNR in the working range of $9$ to $18$\,dB, and still 54 times at $21$\,dB where the
MCS table tops out. The target-aware policy holds URLLC across the whole range. Below
about $6$\,dB the picture worsens in a different way. The lowest MCS is already too
fast for the channel, so the certificate fails even the eMBB consumer it was tuned for,
reaching a BLER of $0.33$ at $3$\,dB. No margin repairs a certificate whose weakest
format is already too aggressive.

\section{The Refresh Horizon}
\label{sec:refresh}
The second axis is time. A report is fresh when issued and ages as the channel
decorrelates. A certificate that was sound at report time false-clears once the channel
has moved on. Here we count actual HARQ NACKs, Eq.~\eqref{eq:fc_emp}. At $200$\,Hz
Doppler, about $62$\,km/h at $3.5$\,GHz, with a report every $20$ TTI, the held
estimate is off by $5.7$\,dB on average. Aging dominates that error; report noise
contributes about $1$\,dB. The naive certificate then runs at a BLER near $0.36$
against a $0.10$ target, a false-clear on roughly a quarter of transmissions. OLLA,
Eq.~\eqref{eq:olla}, corrects the same held report with HARQ feedback and holds
$0.10$. Figure~\ref{fig:refresh}(a) shows the aging across mobility.

The practical question is how often to report. We sweep Doppler from $10$ to $400$\,Hz,
which spans coherence times from $42$\,ms down to $1$\,ms, and report periods from $1$
to $80$ TTI. The refresh floor $P^\star(f_D)$ is the longest period that keeps BLER at
target. A least-squares fit through the origin against the Clarke coherence time
$T_{\mathrm{coh}}=0.423/f_D$ gives
\begin{equation}
P^\star \;\approx\; 0.177\,T_{\mathrm{coh}}, \qquad R^2 = 0.915
\label{eq:ot14}
\end{equation}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:refresh}(b)). The low-mobility points dominate the fit. At high Doppler
the floor stops at one TTI, the shortest period the frame allows, and that stop
matters. Above roughly $200$\,Hz no reporting cadence keeps the naive certificate at
target, so a HARQ-driven correction is no longer an optimization but a requirement.
Below that threshold the fit is a usable rule. Report again before the channel
decorrelates by about $0.18\,T_{\mathrm{coh}}$, rather than on a fixed schedule.

\section{One Budget, Two Fleets, Opposite Verdicts}
\label{sec:flip}
The two axes above share a stronger property: the verdict can invert. Consider two
user fleets whose failures come from different parts of the radio state. The M-fleet
runs at $200$\,Hz Doppler and $12$\,dB mean SNR, so its blocks die of CSI aging. The
S-fleet runs at $10$\,Hz and $7$\,dB, so its blocks die in deep fades. Now take two
margin policies with the same fleet-mean budget of $1.5$\,dB: policy A allocates by
Doppler, policy B allocates the same total by SNR deficit. The M-fleet does better
under A
(NACK rate $0.22$ against $0.36$), % GRADED-SWAP (shakedown; seal 2026-08-27)
and the S-fleet does better under B
($0.16$ against $0.27$), % GRADED-SWAP
on every seed. The fleet-mean NACK rate sits within $0.02$ for the two policies, so
the aggregate cannot order the pair for anyone. A control run sharpens the
mechanism. We read one fleet at two reliability thresholds, a shifted required-SNR
table, and redistributed the margin the same two ways. No inversion appears. A
threshold shift leaves both consumers reading the same projection of the state, and
that is also why \S\ref{sec:urllc} showed dominance rather than inversion. eMBB and
URLLC are a threshold pair. The inversion needs consumers that read different axes
of the state, here mobility against depth, and a mixed cell has both. A scheduler
tuned to one fleet-level number is optimizing something no user experiences.

\section{Related Work}
Link adaptation, CSI aging and outer-loop correction are old subjects. OLLA \cite{olla}
holds a BLER target under imperfect or stale CSI by adjusting an SINR offset from HARQ
feedback. It is exactly the correction our refresh result quantifies. Short-packet
analysis \cite{urllc} establishes the reliability regime where the eMBB-to-URLLC gap
lives. Deployed systems configure CQI reporting at a fixed periodicity
\cite{cqireport}; our refresh floor turns that period into a coherence-time budget
instead. Learned CSI feedback trained on reconstruction error \cite{csinet} is an
active direction. In our own controlled tests the objective gap it might suggest did
not appear at practical operating points, so we claim nothing about it. We do not offer
a better estimator, scheduler or offset rule. We offer the measurement, and the two
dependences and one law it exposes.

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
The unit of correctness in link adaptation is not the CSI report. It is the pair of
report and consumer. Margin and reporting cadence sized to the consumer's target and
coherence time keep a certificate honest. Sized to a nominal channel they do not, and
the cost is measurable: a factor of 112 for a mismatched target, and a coherence-scaled
horizon past which a stale report cannot be saved by any cadence. The evidence stands
on Sionna link-level simulation with real LDPC decoding. Over-the-air HARQ traces are
the natural next step.

The evidence has limits worth stating. It uses one TDL-A profile at $30$\,ns delay
spread, one MCS table topping out at 64-QAM, and one mobility for the target axis. The
refresh law is fit across $10$ to $400$\,Hz, and the target gap is measured across $3$
to $21$\,dB, including the cell edge where the certificate fails its own eMBB consumer.
The factors we left out push the numbers the same way. 256-QAM adds steeper cliffs that
worsen aging, and correlated diversity raises the aware policy's failure rate. On
implementation, the refresh floor needs no new machinery. Event-triggered CSI reporting
with a relative threshold already exists in NR \cite{cqireport} and is an instance of
send-on-delta sampling \cite{sod}. In exploratory runs such a trigger reproduced the
floor constant, near $0.2\,T_{\mathrm{coh}}$, but our sealed confirmation attempt was
voided by a control-sanity check at very low Doppler, so we report that outcome and
claim nothing beyond the prior art. The method itself is not specific to radio. The
same consumer-relative failure shows up in optical quality-of-transmission and in
database replication \cite{otcampaigns}. The results here stand on the NR CSI
certificate alone.
